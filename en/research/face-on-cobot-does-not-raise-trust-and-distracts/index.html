Giving the cobot a face did not raise trust and distracted the people working with it

In an experiment with 40 people, a cobot with a face on its screen earned no more trust than one with a logo and drew the eyes to the screen, taking attention away from the handover area.

Original paper

Impact of Anthropomorphic Robot Design on Trust and Attention in Industrial Human-Robot Interaction

Linda Onnasch, Clara Laudine Hildebrandt

ACM Transactions on Human-Robot Interaction, 2021

DOI 10.1145/3472224

Open access

Why it matters

Many cobots come with eyes or faces on a screen, on the idea that this brings the person closer to the robot. This study indicates that, in industry, a face that conveys nothing about the task can cost attention exactly where hands and robot meet. It matters to anyone who designs or chooses the interface of a collaborative workstation.

Method

The authors ran a preregistered laboratory experiment with 40 students on a simulated assembly task: a Sawyer cobot handed over boxes of parts and the person assembled and checked a board. Half saw on the robot's screen an abstract face with eyes that blinked and changed direction with no relation to the task; the other half saw the company logo. There were 25 handovers and, in the final part, the robot dropped a box. Trust was measured with questionnaires and by the time spent with hands in the handover area, and attention by eye tracking.

Key findings

  • Participants did not perceive the robot with a face as more humanlike than the robot with a logo: the anthropomorphism scores were almost identical (1.66 and 1.67) and low in both groups.
  • The face did not bring more trust. The robot with a face was rated as less reliable (81.15% versus 88.87%), a difference that already appeared before the interaction, just from seeing the robot. Trust grew with experience in both groups.
  • The failure reduced stated trust in both groups. In behavior, only the face group changed: handover time rose from 2.51 s to 3.02 s, while with the logo it hardly varied (2.75 s to 2.82 s). The face did not make people forgive the error, contrary to what the authors expected.
  • With the face, people looked much more at the robot's screen (25.85 fixations versus 8.45) and less at the handover area (47.78 versus 56.26). The effect did not fade over the session.
  • The authors conclude that the humanlike design brought no benefit and should not be applied to industrial robots when it does not support the task, because the distraction may be critical for safety.

Limitations

The sample is small and made up of students, in a laboratory task of about an hour and a half with a single failure; the study does not test real operators or prolonged exposure. Because the face was not perceived as more humanlike, it is unclear whether the lack of effect comes from the design or from the measuring instrument. A group with a blank screen was missing to separate the effect of the face from the effect of the logo.

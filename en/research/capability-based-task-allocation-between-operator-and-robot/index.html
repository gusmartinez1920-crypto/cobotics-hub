Splitting tasks by what each does best, with ergonomics first

The authors propose a three-step procedure for dividing assembly tasks between operator and robot according to each one's capabilities, with ergonomic risk as an exclusion criterion.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Link to the SDGs SDG 8
  • SDG 8 Decent work and economic growth The method's stated goal is to improve the quality of work: tasks that would cause progressive harm to the operator's body go to the robot before any other criterion.

Original paper

Capability-based Task Allocation in Human-robot Collaboration

Fabian Ranz, Vera Hummel, Wilfried Sihn

Procedia Manufacturing, 2017

DOI 10.1016/j.promfg.2017.04.011

Open access · CC BY-NC-ND

Why it matters

In a survey by the authors themselves, deciding where to use collaboration and who does each task emerged as a bigger obstacle than technical problems. The procedure gives a simple path for that decision and makes one priority clear: whatever harms the person's body goes to the robot, regardless of time, cost or quality.

Method

The study has two parts. First, a survey in which 34 respondents, among them 15 robot manufacturers and 14 integrators, rated 17 deployment challenges, plus interviews with 5 user companies. Then the proposed procedure: break the process down into tasks, allocate the tasks with a mandatory decision using an exclusion list of 12 human capabilities and ergonomic limits, and allocate the rest using capability indicators over a catalog of 25 criteria. The proposal was tested qualitatively in two learning factory cases, one in kitting and one in final assembly.

Key findings

  • In the survey, 73% of participants rated as large or very large the challenge of identifying the workstation suited to collaboration and deciding the division of tasks, and 68% said the same of workstation design. Technical challenges, such as reprogramming effort, were not among the top rated.
  • The authors criticize approaches that generalize the capabilities of "robots" and "humans": the robot's capability depends on the equipment installed (force sensor, vision) and the person's on their condition. They cite that dexterity falls by about 30% between the ages of 20 and 60.
  • In the first screening, when criteria conflict, ergonomic risk outweighs capabilities and the task goes to the robot. A task that would cause progressive harm to the person is ruled out for them, regardless of time, cost or quality.
  • The remaining tasks are compared on three dimensions (process time, additional investment and process quality), with a rating of better, equal or worse for each criterion. The weights, equal at the start, can be adjusted.
  • In an example with 8 tasks, four were decided by the exclusion list and four by the capability indicators.

Limitations

The proposal was tested only in learning factories, with no measured results for time, quality or ergonomics; the authors themselves say it still has to prove its practicality in industry. The method considers the person in general, not the individual, and ergonomics enters only as physical risk: mental workload and safety are not allocation criteria.

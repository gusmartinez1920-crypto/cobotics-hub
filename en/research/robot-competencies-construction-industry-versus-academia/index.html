Industry and academia do not prioritize the same competencies for working with robots

Two US expert panels, one from industry and one from academia, rated 42 competencies for human-robot collaboration in construction. The largest gap is in ethics and regulation.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Original paper

Competencies for human–robot collaboration in the construction industry: perspectives from academia and industry

Ebenezer Olukanni, Abiola Akanmu, Houtan Jebelli, Ashtarout Ammar, Akinwale Okunola

Construction Robotics, 2025

DOI 10.1007/s41693-025-00156-y

Open access · CC BY

Why it matters

Anyone building a course or training program on working with robots needs to know whether what universities teach is what companies look for. This study shows where the two sides agree and where they part. The examples are construction robots (painting, 3D printing, drones, autonomous machines), and applying the list to factory cobots is up to the reader.

Method

The authors started from a list of 20 knowledge areas, 10 skills and 12 abilities drawn from the literature. They applied the Delphi method, a consultation of experts in rounds, over three rounds and separately with an industry panel and an academic panel, both in the United States; the last round had 13 and 14 participants. The two groups' ratings were compared with a statistical test, and the priority of each competency was calculated with a relative importance index ranging from 0 to 1.

Key findings

  • Both panels reached internal consensus, and academics gave higher average ratings for almost all competencies.
  • The largest difference is in knowledge of ethics and regulation: 4th place in academia (index of 0.88) and 18th in industry (0.59).
  • In industry, only four knowledge areas reach high importance: human-robot interface (0.84), safety and standards (0.81), robot applications in construction (0.81) and task planning (0.80). In academia there are nine, with interface and safety tied at 0.93.
  • No skill reaches high importance in industry; the first is task planning (0.78). Proficiency with the human-robot interface scores 0.89 for academia and 0.51 for industry.
  • Among abilities, teamwork comes first in both panels (0.76 and 0.87) and cultural and social awareness comes last. The authors interpret that industry prioritizes immediate operational needs and academia theoretical foundations, and they argue for collaboration between the two.

Limitations

The panels are small, lost participants between rounds and bring together managers, engineers and professors; the site worker who operates next to the robot was not heard. The results apply to construction in the United States and do not distinguish robot types or experience levels, which the authors leave for future research.

The risks of working with a cobot go far beyond collisions

A review of 32 documents gathered 254 risk factors for human-robot collaboration and sorted them into five classes: human, technology, workspace, enterprise and external.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

  • SDG 8 Decent work and economic growth The study shows that the safety of people working with cobots includes stress, trust and mental workload, not only the risk of collision.

Original paper

Identification and classification of risk factors for human-robot collaboration from a system-wide perspective

Nicole Berx, Wilm Decré, Ido Morag, Peter Chemweno, Liliane Pintelon

Computers & Industrial Engineering, 2022

DOI 10.1016/j.cie.2021.107827

Open access

Why it matters

People who install a cobot often assess the risk of physical contact and stop there. This study offers a broader list that can guide risk assessment early in the design: it includes what happens to the person, to the way work is organized and to the data.

Method

The authors carried out a state-of-the-art review in two article databases (Web of Science and Scopus), covering 2011 to early 2021, and added documents from European bodies and ISO standards, for a total of 32 sources. They extracted every risk factor mentioned, grouped the factors into classes over three rounds of comparison, and checked the result against models of sociotechnical systems.

Key findings

  • They found 254 risk factors, organized into five classes: human, technology, collaborative workspace, enterprise and external.
  • Technology accounts for 44% of the factors and human for 27%. Only two of the documents analyzed cover all five classes at once, which points to little research on the system as a whole.
  • In the human class, more than half of the factors are psychosocial: too little or too much trust in the robot, human error and stress. These factors appear in 62% of the contributing documents, even though the search used no psychosocial terms.
  • The enterprise class includes ethics (acceptance, privacy and surveillance, algorithmic decisions) and work organization (lack of training, deskilling, job design).
  • None of the papers surveyed addresses operator participation in risk assessment, which the authors point to as a topic for research.

Limitations

The study identifies and classifies the factors but does not measure how important each one is: counting factors does not show which are more serious, as the authors themselves acknowledge. The classification has not yet been validated in factories, and the review predates the 2025 edition of the ISO 10218 standard.

A taxonomy to describe interaction between people and robots in construction

Based on a review that narrowed down to 326 studies, the authors propose a taxonomy of 16 categories in three dimensions (team, task and environment) to describe human-robot interaction in construction.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

Original paper

A multidimensional taxonomy for human-robot interaction in construction

Patrick B. Rodrigues, Rashmi Singh, Mert Oytun, Pooya Adami, Peter J. Woods, Burcin Becerik-Gerber, Lucio Soibelman, Yasemin Copur-Gencturk, Gale M. Lucas

Automation in Construction, 2023

DOI 10.1016/j.autcon.2023.104845

Open access

Why it matters

Without a shared vocabulary, it is hard to compare studies and projects involving robots. The taxonomy offers a grid to describe who does what, with which robot, on which task and where. It was built for construction, but the human roles, levels of autonomy and safety mechanisms it lists are also a useful reference for people working with cobots in other sectors.

Method

The authors carried out a qualitative systematic review in two databases (Web of Science and Scopus), with no sector restriction, covering 1985 to 2022. From 7,794 records they reached 326 empirical studies on physical tasks with robots. Five raters built the categories and measured agreement among themselves over nine rounds. They then validated the taxonomy with 97 randomly selected construction papers and compared it with four existing taxonomies.

Key findings

  • The taxonomy has 16 categories in three dimensions. Team gathers characteristics of the person, of the robot and shared ones; task covers type, planning, and training and learning; environment covers the setting and the robot's location.
  • There are five human roles: operator, supervisor, collaborator, cooperator (works alongside, on independent parts of the task) and bystander, who only shares the space with the robot.
  • For the robot, the taxonomy adopts four levels of autonomy (teleoperation, shared control, full autonomy and sliding autonomy) and five types: stationary, mobile, swarm, humanoid and exoskeleton.
  • Safety mechanisms are grouped into five families: control, motion planning, prediction, hardware and psychological factors, the last aimed at reducing discomfort and stress.
  • Agreement among raters ranged from 0.62 to 0.92: higher in objective categories, such as setting and task type, and lower in those that depend on interpretation, such as robot and team characteristics.

Limitations

The taxonomy deliberately leaves out sensors, end effectors and control mechanisms, and received no input from roboticists or industrial engineers, as the authors acknowledge. Safety appears only as a robot mechanism: there is no category for risk assessment, applicable standards or worker health. The concept of robot is broad and includes teleoperated machines, exoskeletons and drones.

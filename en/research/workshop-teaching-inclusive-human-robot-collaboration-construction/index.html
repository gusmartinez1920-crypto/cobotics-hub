A four-day workshop to teach cobot design for diverse users

In a workshop with 17 master's students, the authors tested a way of teaching human-robot collaboration for construction, guided by questions about inclusion and by user feedback.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Original paper

Designing for diversity: a feminist technoscience and behavioural fabrication approach in human–robot collaboration education for Industry 5.0

Gili Ron, Samuel Leder, Lasath Siriwardena, Cordula Kropp, Achim Menges, Thomas Wortmann

Construction Robotics, 2025

DOI 10.1007/s41693-025-00164-y

Open access · CC BY

Why it matters

Teaching human-robot collaboration tends to favor technique and overlook who will use the system. This study describes a hands-on class format that can be reproduced: a simple cobot demonstrator, role rotation and a list of questions for assessing the design. It also shows where voice and vision interfaces fail outside ideal conditions.

Method

A teaching case study: a four-day workshop at an architecture institute in Barcelona, with 17 master's students in architecture, design and engineering, in four groups. The students first built a tower of wooden blocks, taking turns with a UR10e cobot that did not react to them, and then created new robot behaviors using sensors. The authors assessed each project with a rubric of four dimensions, with four questions each, drawn from a feminist technoscience framework.

Key findings

  • The workshop produced four interaction projects: two-way voice commands, hand gestures with speed reduction when a person approaches, assembly checking by computer vision and speech, and a multilingual voice interface designed for migrant workers.
  • The gesture and speed-reduction project received the best rubric assessment. The participation and reflection dimension scored low overall, which the authors attribute to the short time frame.
  • Ambient noise was the main failure of the voice-based projects, with delays and language identification errors, and no group built an alternative input mode. Computer vision only worked with special lighting and colored tape on the blocks.
  • Three of the four groups chose language-based interaction, even though the system had been designed for vision data.
  • Some groups repeated the command-and-execute logic and treated the cobot as a subordinate. When communication failed, responsibility went back to the person, with no shared recovery mechanism.

Limitations

No construction workers took part: the students were both users and testers, and only two groups tested with outsiders, three volunteers in total. The rubric was applied by the authors themselves, and there are no measures of performance, workload or trust. The evaluation was short and does not capture fatigue or stress.

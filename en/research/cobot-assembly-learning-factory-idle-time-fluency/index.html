A person and a cobot working at the same time shorten the assembly cycle

In a learning factory in Croatia, the authors compared three ways of splitting an assembly task between a person and a cobot and measured idle time, concurrent work and delay.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

Original paper

Integration of the human-robot system in the learning factory assembly process

Amanda Aljinovic, Marina Crnjac, Gjeldum Nikola, Marko Mladineo, Andrej Basic, Veza Ivica

Procedia Manufacturing, 2020

DOI 10.1016/j.promfg.2020.04.088

Open access · CC BY-NC-ND

Why it matters

The study uses a simple case to show how to measure whether the division of tasks between a person and a cobot is flowing well. The four measures used (robot idle time, human idle time, concurrent activity and delay) can be reproduced in a teaching lab or at a real workstation before deciding where to install the robot.

Method

A case study at the first station of a manual assembly line in the learning factory of the University of Split, which produces a small children's vehicle. A Panda cobot from Franka Emika positions the axle and places the wheels; the person fits washers and nuts and fastens the axle to the base plate. The authors measured active and inactive times in three variants (robot first, person first and simultaneous operations) and calculated four objective fluency measures. They then increased the speed of the arm.

Key findings

  • With the robot working first and the person afterward, there is no concurrent work: the robot is idle for 50.43% of the task time and the person for 49.56%.
  • With the person working first, a delay of 4 seconds is added to the total time, caused by the person moving within the workstation and by the time the robot takes to process the start signal.
  • With simultaneous operations, total time drops: robot idle time falls to 31.33%, human idle time to 30.12%, and 38.55% of the time becomes joint activity, with no delay.
  • With the arm speed set to the maximum the workstation allowed, station time fell to 65 seconds and concurrent activity reached 49.23%. Human idle time rose to 40%, which the authors attribute to the shorter total time.
  • The authors note that the four measures are interrelated: improving one can worsen another.

Limitations

The study looks only at efficiency: it does not assess how the operator perceives pace, workload or comfort, and it includes no risk assessment when the arm speed is increased. It is a single case and does not report how many measurements were taken or who operated the station. Although it takes place in a learning factory, it does not describe any teaching activity or learning outcomes.

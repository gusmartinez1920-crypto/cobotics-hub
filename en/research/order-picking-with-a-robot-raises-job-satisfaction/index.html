Picking orders with a robot raised satisfaction, even more when the person follows the robot

In an experiment with 60 students in a laboratory warehouse, picking orders with a mobile robot raised satisfaction compared with manual picking. The effect was larger when the person followed the robot.

Original paper

Assessing the impact of human–robot collaborative order picking systems on warehouse workers

Alexandros Pasparakis, Jelle De Vries, René De Koster

International Journal of Production Research, 2023

DOI 10.1080/00207543.2023.2183343

Open access · CC BY-NC-ND

Why it matters

It is common to assume that giving up autonomy to the machine makes workers less satisfied. This experiment found the opposite, at least in the short term, and shows that how useful the person finds the robot weighs on satisfaction. It matters to anyone designing how control is shared between person and robot in logistics.

Method

The authors ran a controlled experiment with 60 students from a vocational logistics college in the Netherlands, with an average age of 18. In a laboratory warehouse with 300 pick locations, each participant picked orders in three ways, in 20-minute rounds: by hand with a cart, leading an autonomous mobile robot, and following the robot. Questionnaires measured job satisfaction, self-esteem, confidence in one's own ability and confidence in interacting with robots. The analysis used statistical models that account for repeated measures from each person.

Key findings

  • Job satisfaction rose in both robot setups. The averages, on a scale of 1 to 5, were 3.45 for manual picking, 3.62 when the person led the robot and 3.88 when the person followed the robot.
  • Following the robot also raised self-esteem, contrary to the authors' hypothesis; leading the robot had no effect. General confidence in one's own ability did not change in either setup.
  • Confidence in interacting with robots increased in both setups, to a similar degree, even after short training.
  • The more useful the person found the robot, the higher the satisfaction. The authors conclude that workers may be less satisfied if they are required to work with robots they do not see as useful.
  • To explain the higher satisfaction when following the robot, the authors propose that it guides the person to the right location, reduces mental load and the chance of error, and keeps a steady pace.

Limitations

The measures were taken right after 20-minute rounds; the study does not show what happens in long-term collaboration and does not measure turnover, which motivated the research. The sample consists of young students, mostly men with little experience, in a laboratory. Workload, fatigue and the pace set by the robot were not measured, and the robot is a mobile vehicle that carries the box, not a collaborative arm.

How to adapt hazard identification to Industry 5.0

A review of 40 studies proposes adapting hazard identification (HAZID) to Industry 5.0 and sorts the new risks into three groups: information security, human-robot interaction and technology integration.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Original paper

Advancing occupational and system safety in Industry 5.0: Effective HAZID, risk analysis frameworks, and human-AI interaction management

Kamran Gholamizadeh, Esmaeil Zarei, Luca Gualtieri, Matteo De Marchi

Safety Science, 2025

DOI 10.1016/j.ssci.2024.106770

Why it matters

Collaborative robot standards deal mainly with mechanical hazards. Anyone designing a cell with cobots, AI and connected machines also needs to look at cybersecurity, human error and decisions made by algorithms. The review offers step-by-step protocols that can serve as a starting point.

Method

The authors carried out a systematic literature review in three databases (Scopus, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar), with searches up to May 2024, and included 40 studies in the synthesis. Three researchers screened the studies independently. The synthesis is narrative: the authors group the themes, propose protocols in tables and illustrate them with two hypothetical cases.

Key findings

  • The authors argue for adapting hazard identification (HAZID) rather than replacing it, because it is an established method that safety professionals already know. The proposed protocol has four steps: understand the context, assess the challenges, integrate solutions and improve continuously.
  • According to the review, cobots can help hazard identification, not only make it harder, by providing real-time data on operating conditions and on interaction with people.
  • Emerging risks are sorted into three categories: information security, occupational risks in human-robot interaction, and technology integration risks. Connectivity creates cyber risks that can become a physical hazard for people.
  • ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 cover mainly mechanical hazards. For the rest, the authors point to ISO/IEC 27001, ISO/IEC 27005 and ISO 45001.
  • Human errors are classified into five types (cognitive, procedural, fatigue-related, technological and complacency), each with a prevention strategy. The review also notes a lack of consensus on how to identify the risks of AI systems used for decision-making.

Limitations

The protocols are the authors' proposals and have not been tested: the two cases presented are hypothetical and have no measured data. The authors themselves acknowledge that long-term data and comparisons across sectors are missing. The cobot appears as one of several technologies, with no discussion of collaborative modes or of force and pressure limits.

A learning factory in Hungary dedicated to human-robot collaboration

The authors describe a learning factory under construction in Győr, Hungary, with assembly stations equipped with cobots, and the plan to use it in courses and in projects with industry.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Original paper

Human–robot collaboration in the MTA SZTAKI learning factory facility at Győr

Zsolt Kemény, Richárd Beregi, János Nacsa, Csaba Kardos, Dániel Horváth

Procedia Manufacturing, 2018

DOI 10.1016/j.promfg.2018.04.001

Open access · CC BY-NC-ND

Why it matters

Anyone planning a cobot laboratory for teaching will find design decisions already thought through here: independent cells, a reconfigurable layout and conventional equipment alongside for comparison. The text also points out that knowing automation is not enough to decide when collaboration is worthwhile.

Method

A description of a facility still under construction, with no data collection and no evaluation with students. The text presents the purpose and design principles, the components (four stations with UR5 or UR10 cobots, a fifth for dual-arm assembly and two autonomous vehicles, one of them carrying a UR3 cobot) and the plan for use on two tracks, industrial and educational. The area comprises 150 m² of shop floor and 75 m² of rooms.

Key findings

  • The authors note that the ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066 standards already address the safety of the whole set of robot, tool, workpiece and task, but that the mental, emotional and social aspects of collaboration receive little space in standards.
  • The design favors layout reconfiguration, independent cells linked by internal transport instead of a conveyor, and the possibility of collaboration both between person and robot and between robots.
  • The facility shares its space with a university's conventional equipment, which allows side-by-side comparison of collaborative and non-collaborative solutions for similar tasks.
  • The work surfaces do not yield on contact and only the robots have built-in safety limits. For this reason, a supervisor must assess the safety of each station configuration before it goes into use.
  • The teaching plan follows an assembly problem in four steps: product design, feasibility tests of the operations, layout of the station and the process with collaboration where it is beneficial, and production.

Limitations

This is a plan, not a result: there is no number of students, no target competencies and no assessment of learning. The user interfaces and the digital twin were still under development. The planned teaching serves students of technical management and mechanical engineering, not the people who will operate the stations.

Teleoperated ultrasound with a cobot: the haptic interface performs better but tires more

The study compared two interfaces for teleoperating a cobot in an ultrasound exam. The haptic device gave less error and shorter times than the 3D mouse but demanded more effort.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

Original paper

Design and Quantitative Assessment of Teleoperation-Based Human–Robot Collaboration Method for Robot-Assisted Sonography

Weiyong Si, Ning Wang, Chenguang Yang

IEEE Transactions on Automation Science and Engineering, 2025

DOI 10.1109/tase.2024.3350524

Open access

Why it matters

Choosing a teleoperation interface is often a cost decision made without usage data. This study proposes a set of objective and subjective measures for comparing interfaces and shows a common tension in collaborative robotics: the best-performing option may be the one that loads the operator most.

Method

The authors built a teleoperation system in which the operator drives a Franka Emika Panda cobot holding an ultrasound probe, using either a haptic device (Touch X) or a low-cost 3D mouse. The robot uses impedance control, which makes it compliant in contact. Six people scanned a training phantom while trying to keep a contact force of 10 N. The measures were perceived workload (NASA-TLX questionnaire), learning and execution times, force error, image quality and muscle fatigue by electromyography.

Key findings

  • With the haptic device the force error was 3.8 N, against 7.4 N with the 3D mouse, and the mean task time was 53 s against 84 s.
  • The haptic device also had a smaller probe orientation error and less image noise. On the other hand, it takes longer to learn.
  • The muscle fatigue index was higher with the haptic device (89.34 against 76.23). In the questionnaire, mean mental demand was almost twice that of the 3D mouse (13.4 against 6.8).
  • The low stiffness of the controller makes contact with a person gentler but causes errors and delays in tracking position and orientation. The authors treat this as an inherent limit of this type of control.
  • The authors conclude that the haptic device outperforms the 3D mouse in everything except muscle fatigue, and propose shared control and machine learning to relieve the operator.

Limitations

The sample has six participants, the tests were run on a training phantom rather than on patients, and the differences were not tested statistically. The study does not analyze force limits for patient safety.

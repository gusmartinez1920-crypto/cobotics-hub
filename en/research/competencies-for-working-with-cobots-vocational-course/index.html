What operators and engineers need to know to work with cobots

Based on 60 interviews at 21 Dutch factories, the authors listed 31 competencies for working with cobots and designed a 240-hour vocational course for operators.

Original paper

A Collaborative Robot in the Classroom: Designing 21st Century Engineering Education Together

Milan Wolffgramm, Tom Tijink, Mirte Disberg-Van Geloven, Stephan Corporaal

Journal of Higher Education Theory and Practice, 2021

DOI 10.33423/jhetp.v21i16.4924

Open access

Why it matters

Teachers who want to bring a cobot into the classroom often have no reference for what to teach. This study starts from what industry actually does and arrives at two training profiles, operator and programmer, with a course design that can be adapted: classroom lessons, practice with the robot, a workshop and assessment.

Method

The study has two stages. In the first, the authors reanalyzed 60 interviews conducted at 21 Dutch manufacturers with cobot experience, with 29 engineers, 11 line managers and 20 shop-floor workers; three researchers classified the statements using a standardized model for describing occupations. In the second, a group of teachers from two vocational schools, researchers, educational designers, company professionals and an integrator designed the course, which was tested in a pilot with 5 students.

Key findings

  • The authors found 31 competencies, in four groups: design, programming, operation and repair.
  • The division of roles in the factories is the classic one. The engineer decides on the application, installs, programs and solves complex errors; the worker operates the cobot and solves errors by following detailed instructions.
  • For operation, the worker needs mechanical knowledge, time management (almost all of them do other tasks while the cobot runs), spatial awareness to avoid collisions and manual dexterity. For repair, the worker can only restart the cobot and reselect the program; if that does not work, the engineer is called.
  • Instead of creating a new course, the group adapted a 240-hour elective on industrial robots. The course has three parts: classroom lessons, practice with the cobot (including activation, safety and repair exercises) and a workshop with packaging and joint assembly stations.
  • Assessment combines a written exam (30%), a practical exam (50%) and a reflection interview (20%). In the pilot, the basic tasks were too easy, and the cobot's errors served as learning opportunities.

Limitations

The course had not yet been delivered: the pilot had only 5 students and did not measure learning. Only the operator course was designed; the programmer course remained a list of contents. The interviews are all from Dutch factories, and the study describes the existing division of roles without discussing whether the operator should have more autonomy.

Laying 13 kg bricks with a cobot guided through augmented reality

A prototype used augmented reality so a person could guide a mobile cobot laying 13 kg bricks in a wall. The mean deviation from the design was about 34 mm.

Summary by Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Português · Español

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

Original paper

Enhanced interactive AR bricklaying: elevating human–robot collaboration in augmented reality assisted masonry design and construction

Weichen Zhang, Pierpaolo Ruttico

Architectural Intelligence, 2025

DOI 10.1007/s44223-025-00085-4

Open access · CC BY

Why it matters

The study takes the cobot out of the factory and into construction, and tests how much human decision-making fits into the execution phase. It shows a trade-off found in any collaborative application: more human intervention brings flexibility and spares part of the planning, but costs precision and efficiency. It also warns that a gesture interface can leave people out.

Method

The work has two stages. In the first, an experiment with small wooden blocks and a UR10e cobot compared two modes: designing everything in augmented reality and then building, or alternating design and construction layer by layer; the assessment was qualitative, based on observation by four researchers. In the second, the authors built a full-scale wall of 13 kg bricks at an exhibition, using a Fanuc CRX-25iA cobot on a mobile base, an augmented reality headset, gesture commands and a camera to correct localization. They measured the deviation of each brick and ran demonstrations with about 20 construction professionals.

Key findings

  • In the experiment, designing everything beforehand gave more precision and efficiency but involved the user little. Alternating design and construction involved the user more, with unpredictable precision and low efficiency. In the final case, the shape is designed beforehand and adjusted during construction.
  • Human guidance through augmented reality replaced the structural stability simulation, when complexity is low, and automatic path planning with obstacle avoidance.
  • Most bricks ended up within 25 mm of the design position, and the mean deviation was about 34 mm. The largest deviations were in the stretch with the greatest curvature, which the user reduced on their own judgment to keep the wall stable.
  • Manual adjustment of brick position is not very precise: bricks placed too high or too low can stop the robot because of excessive force.
  • Most of the roughly 20 professionals used the system after a brief introduction. Those who could not perform the gestures did not complete the task, and those who failed were older; the authors suggest a handheld controller as an alternative.

Limitations

This is an exhibition prototype, with no formal protocol, time measurement or questionnaire: the assessment of the experience comes from the authors' own observation and informal opinions. Many bricks were laid by hand because of the robot's height limit. The study states that the robot complies with the standards but presents no risk assessment of the application.

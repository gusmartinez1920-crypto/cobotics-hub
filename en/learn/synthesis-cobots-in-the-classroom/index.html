Cobots in the classroom: what the research shows about training

Nine summaries on how to train people who work with cobots: operator and engineer skills, teaching factories, training benches, and how little learning has been measured so far.

By Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Español · Português

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

The nine summaries in this topic answer in two parts. On who gets trained, the most concrete reference comes from Dutch factories: a list of skills and a vocational course for the operator (Wolffgramm et al., 2021). On the classroom, the texts describe teaching factories, benches, and educational robots built around real assembly tasks. They share one weak point: almost none of them measured how much people learn.

What to teach depends on who operates and who programs

From 60 interviews in 21 factories, the authors listed 31 skills in four groups: design, programming, operation, and repair. The division of roles is the classic one: the engineer decides on the application, programs, and solves the complex errors; the worker operates the cobot and needs mechanical knowledge, time management, spatial awareness, and manual dexterity (Wolffgramm et al., 2021). The operator course adapted a 240-hour elective on industrial robots, with classroom lessons, hands-on practice with the cobot, and a workshop. In the pilot, with only 5 students, the basic tasks were too easy (Wolffgramm et al., 2021). An account from Volvo adds a warning: the technology raises the demands on workers and may be an obstacle for older, experienced ones (Götvall, 2019).

What the classroom takes from the factory: a whole assembly problem

A teaching factory planned in Hungary organizes the course around an assembly problem in four stages, from product design to production. The cells are independent, the layout can be reconfigured, and conventional equipment stands alongside, so collaborative and non-collaborative solutions can be compared (Kemény et al., 2018). On a smaller scale, a portable bench from Slovakia proposes an exercise that carries its own assessment: the student calculates the gear ratio, programs the cobot, assembles the gears with it, and tests; only some combinations make the mechanism move (Kovaľuk et al., 2022).

At a teaching factory station in Croatia, the authors compared three ways of splitting the assembly using four measures: robot idle time, human idle time, concurrent activity, and delay. With simultaneous operations, 38.55% of the time became joint activity, with no delay (Aljinovic et al., 2020).

Touching the robot raised confidence; the knowledge gain is less clear

In a pilot with 22 adults with no engineering background, a cobot delivered a 20-minute course on its own. Those who touched and moved the arm gained more self-efficacy, that is, confidence in their own ability, than those who watched the same lesson on video (Selby et al., 2021). For knowledge, the difference between the groups did not reach the usual significance level (Selby et al., 2021). In Chile, 34 children aged 4 to 6 commanded a cobot by voice for one week; the mean score on a computational thinking test rose from 8.76 to 10.12, out of 15 points, with no control group (Castro et al., 2024).

Making the lab cheaper does not remove the need to look after safety

A 3D-printed arm for teaching was tested with only three of its six axes, and its neural-network collision detection failed (Ananias & Gaspar, 2022). In virtual reality, adding a real workbench to the scene did not significantly increase the sense of presence in a test with 12 students, and the mismatch between image and object got in the way (Sievers et al., 2021). With real robots, safety falls to whoever teaches: in the Hungarian factory, a supervisor assesses each station configuration before use (Kemény et al., 2018); with the children, the area was marked off, an adult stood between the child and the robot, and the speed was reduced (Castro et al., 2024).

What is still unknown

The biggest gap is learning. Four texts describe facilities, benches, prototypes, or partnerships with no assessed students and no outcome data (Kemény et al., 2018; Kovaľuk et al., 2022; Ananias & Gaspar, 2022; Götvall, 2019). The studies that did measure something are small and short: 11 people per group in a single session (Selby et al., 2021); children with no control group (Castro et al., 2024); university students assessed only on sense of presence (Sievers et al., 2021). None of them measured the ability to work with the cobot on real tasks. The fluency study did not ask the operator and did not assess risk when raising the arm’s speed (Aljinovic et al., 2020).

In practice

  • Separate the operator and programmer profiles and start from a course that already exists (Wolffgramm et al., 2021).
  • Organize practice around a complete assembly task, with adjustable difficulty and a test that shows whether the solution works (Kovaľuk et al., 2022; Kemény et al., 2018).
  • Use idle time, concurrent activity, and delay to evaluate the division of tasks at a teaching station (Aljinovic et al., 2020).
  • Let the learner touch and move the robot when the goal is to build confidence; on recall questions, video had an equivalent result (Selby et al., 2021).
  • With low-cost robots and mixed reality, check collision detection and image alignment before using them with students (Ananias & Gaspar, 2022; Sievers et al., 2021).

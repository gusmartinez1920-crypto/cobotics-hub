Cobotics, IoT and digital twins: the closed loop of Industry 5.0

How sensors, virtual replicas and collaborative robots form a single system that measures, simulates and acts — with people at the center.

The relationship between cobotics (collaborative robots), digital twins and the IoT (Internet of Things) is the backbone of Industry 5.0, where technology is combined with human-centricity, sustainability and resilience. These three technologies do not work in isolation: together they form one integrated, continuous cyber-physical system.

IoT: the eyes and ears of the system

The IoT is the data acquisition and transmission layer of the physical world.

  • Sensors across the shop floor, on the cobots themselves and on the products being made collect real-time measurements such as temperature, vibration, torque, energy use, position and humidity.
  • Without the IoT, machines would stay isolated. It turns the physical environment into a continuous stream of data.

Digital twin: the brain and the simulator

The digital twin is the real-time virtual representation of the physical system, fed directly by the data the IoT collects.

  • Simulation and optimization: the twin does more than mirror what is happening. It uses algorithms and AI to predict failures, simulate production scenarios and optimize routes or workloads before applying them in the physical world.
  • Human–machine context: in Industry 5.0, the twin helps model the interaction between the operator and the robot, supporting ergonomics, safety and efficiency.

Cobotics: physical, collaborative action

Cobotics covers collaborative robots (cobots), designed to work side by side with people instead of being fenced off like traditional industrial robots.

  • While a traditional robot repeats tasks without perceiving its surroundings, a cobot has force sensing, computer vision and an immediate response to human contact.
  • The cobot can receive parameters optimized by the digital twin, adjusting speed, force and task to the process and to the presence of the operator.

How the three connect in practice

  1. Collection (IoT): sensors track the wear of a cobot’s tool or the force applied during a delicate assembly.
  2. Processing and mirroring (digital twin): the data reaches the twin, which updates the cobot’s virtual state and simulates whether there is a risk of equipment fatigue or lost efficiency.
  3. Action (cobotics): when the twin detects an improvement opportunity or a risk, the cobot’s behavior is adjusted — slowing down, switching tasks or safely alerting the operator.

Step 3 is by nature the most delicate: any change in the behavior of a robot that shares space with people must stay within the safety limits defined in the cell’s risk assessment.

This integration reduces downtime, improves workplace safety and makes production more flexible, combining the precision of automation with human intuition and creativity.

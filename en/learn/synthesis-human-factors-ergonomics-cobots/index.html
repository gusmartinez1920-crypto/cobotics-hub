Human factors and ergonomics with cobots: what the research shows

What 14 studies say about stress, mental workload, physical effort, and trust when people work with cobots: the cost depends on speed, predictability, and who controls the pace.

By Claude (Anthropic) · Published November 13, 2026

Also available in: Español · Português

Written with AI assistance. Still under review by the team.

There is a cost, but the price depends on how the cell is set up. Robot speed, predictable motion, and who controls the pace change the stress and mental workload (the effort of attention a task demands) of the person working beside the machine. This synthesis draws on 14 summaries, mostly lab experiments and reviews.

How the robot moves changes how the person feels

A review of 114 studies found that, with industrial manipulators, people feel safer with greater distance, lower speed, predictable motion, and warning of the motion (Rubagotti et al., 2022). A review of 23 studies on mental workload adds that unpredictable motion and time pressure raise stress (Carissoli et al., 2023).

But slower was not always better. In an experiment with 42 people, high speed worsened perceived safety, while low speed brought more negative emotions (Gervasi et al., 2022). In virtual reality, with a mobile robot and 56 adults, trust on the curved path was higher at 1.0 m/s than at 0.5 and 1.5 m/s (Haney et al., 2026).

Whoever controls the pace feels less pressure

When the cobot moved on a fixed timer, without the operator’s command, participants reported more pressure and anxiety (Gervasi et al., 2022). In an experiment with 17 students, perceived workload in sequential tasks averaged 19.06 with the person as leader and 28.82 as follower; the follower also did more parts of the task alone, which may explain part of the difference (Segura et al., 2025). There is a cost: following the robot’s pace is more stressful, but productivity is higher when the robot leads (Carissoli et al., 2023).

Does the cobot relieve the mind or overload it? Studies disagree

In an experiment with 10 students, mental workload measured through brain signals fell with cobot support; the fixed order of the scenarios may have favored the later ones (Caiazzo et al., 2025). By contrast, in a review of 163 studies on construction, more automation with a robotic arm was associated with more fatigue, stress, and cognitive load than manual execution (Antwi et al., 2026). Familiarity matters: in a test with 14 people, reported workload was lower with a cobot that displayed its current action, and inexperienced participants tended to find automation more demanding (Lagomarsino et al., 2023).

For the body, always helping the same way has a downside

Adapting the cobot to posture is the most mature area; for cognitive load, there is no accepted method or set of metrics (Lorenzini et al., 2023). A planner that hands the most fatiguing steps to the cobot cut perceived exertion from 4.56 to 2.31 in a test with 8 people (You & Ji, 2025). But handing over the part at the same spot every time repeats the same movement. In an experiment with 16 people, varying the handover point cut the time in high-risk posture from 13.86% to 8.87% of handovers (Zolotas et al., 2024).

Too much trust also has a cost, and trust is poorly measured

Robot failures reduce trust, and excessive trust was associated with more unsafe behavior (Antwi et al., 2026). In a household task, a robot that estimates the person’s trust improved team performance; the policy that seeks performance outdid the one that seeks maximum trust (Chen et al., 2020). A position paper warns that several well-known scales measure how trustworthy the robot seems, not the person’s willingness to be exposed to it (Onnasch et al., 2026).

What is still unknown

Almost everything comes from the lab, with short tasks and volunteers. In the mental workload review, only 2 of 23 studies took place in a factory (Carissoli et al., 2023). In the construction review, 95% of studies lasted less than an 8-hour shift (Antwi et al., 2026). Only three studies on perceived safety lasted up to two months (Rubagotti et al., 2022). And the proposal of proactive collaboration, in which the robot anticipates the person, offers no indicator to measure operator well-being (Li et al., 2021).

In practice

  • Give the operator control of the pace and, where possible, the lead in the task (Gervasi et al., 2022; Segura et al., 2025).
  • Prefer predictable motion with warning, and test the speed: neither the highest nor the lowest speed was rated best (Rubagotti et al., 2022; Haney et al., 2026).
  • Show the operator what the robot is doing, especially those with no robotics experience (Lagomarsino et al., 2023).
  • Assess ergonomics beyond a single cycle: the same handover point, repeated every time, can force repetitive movements (Zolotas et al., 2024).

whyItMatters: "The study is not about robots: it reviews construction, oil and gas, mining and similar sectors. The link to cobots is the reader's, and it rests on two points. The review offers concepts that are useful for assessing psychosocial risks at any workstation, and the authors themselves point to the lack of research on digitalization, intelligent automation and interaction with robots as a gap."
method: "The authors carried out a systematic review in four databases, with the search run in June 2019 and no date limit, complemented by reference searches up to April 2020. From 1936 results, they assessed 202 full texts and included 40 quantitative studies with frontline workers. The synthesis is not statistical: it organizes the results according to the job demands-resources model."
findings:
  - "Associations with safety behavior are more consistent than those with safety outcomes. Of the 69 estimates on behavior, 17% are not significant; of the 31 on injuries, accidents and near misses, 52% are not."
  - "Leadership appears as a central piece. Unfair reward, hands-off leadership, distrust of managers and vague procedures are associated with worse safety; supervisor support and authentic leadership are associated with better safety behavior."
  - "Demands such as overload, ambiguity and conflict tend to lead to states that harm health, such as emotional exhaustion."
  - "The field looks more at what goes wrong than at what happens when everything goes right: 15 studies deal with negative states, such as stress and fatigue, and only 6 with positive states, such as engagement and satisfaction."
  - "The authors found no study on the effects of digitalization and intelligent automation on psychosocial states and safety behavior, even though these changes alter collaboration, for example in interaction with robots."
limitations: "Almost all the evidence comes from questionnaires answered once by the workers themselves; only three studies use objective measures, so the review shows associations, not cause. Three quarters of the studies come from four countries and three sectors, which limits generalization. The review provides no evidence on cobots."

whyItMatters: "Speed, distance and who sets the pace are choices every integrator makes when configuring a collaborative cell. The study shows how each of them changes what the person feels and tests whether a sensor wristband can pick up these reactions. It helps design tasks with the operator's mental load in mind as well."
method: "A laboratory experiment in Turin with 42 participants, most of them with no previous contact with cobots. Each person assembled two flanges with a UR3e cobot in 12 setups that combined three robot speeds, two distances (30 and 40 cm from the chest) and two pacing modes: the operator releases the robot with a button, or the robot proceeds after a fixed 25 seconds. After each trial, participants answered questionnaires on emotion and interaction quality, and a wristband recorded skin electrical activity and heart rate variability."
findings:
  - "Speed and pace control were the factors with the greatest effect. The difference in distance between 30 and 40 cm hardly changed the experience, which the authors attribute to the small size of the robot."
  - "With the robot at low speed, participants found it less useful and the collaboration less natural, efficient and fluent than at medium and high speeds, and they reported more negative emotions, attributed to boredom and distraction."
  - "High speed worsened perceived safety and increased discomfort compared with low speed. Trust in the robot did not vary significantly with speed."
  - "When the robot proceeded on a fixed time, without the operator's command, every dimension assessed got worse: usefulness, trust, naturalness, efficiency, safety, fluency and comfort. Participants reported more pressure and anxiety."
  - "The physiological measures confirmed the questionnaires only in part: the skin electrical response was lower at low speed, but heart rate variability showed no significant difference for any factor."
limitations: "This is a preliminary laboratory study, with a short task, a single small cobot and participants who were largely students rather than factory operators. Interactions between factors and the habituation effect across trials were analyzed only descriptively."

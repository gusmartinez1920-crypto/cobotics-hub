whyItMatters: "The essay is not about cobots or factories: the link to collaborative robotics is the reader's own. Even so, it offers useful ideas for anyone deploying robots at work, such as giving more weight to those most exposed to risk and treating technical standards as texts that can be contested by those who feel their effects."
method: "A single-author philosophical essay, with no data. It starts from two strands of the literature on robot ethics, formulates three problems and discusses them with support from Levinas and Derrida. It illustrates the argument with hypothetical scenarios in three areas (care robotics, autonomous vehicles and algorithmic governance) and compares it with ethical pluralism and with the debate on responsibility gaps."
findings:
  - "The author identifies three problems. First, ethics fragments when every interaction is treated as a unique case. Second, it is unclear who is answerable: the robot or those who design, deploy and profit from it. Third, law requires general rules, while justice calls for attention to the singular case."
  - "The essay takes up the criticism that robots can serve as instruments of control over people, in regimes of efficiency and surveillance such as those of warehouses and assembly lines."
  - "As a practical response, the author proposes default rules that preserve predictability, combined with points of interruption: justification requirements, audit trails, contestation procedures and impact assessments."
  - "The author argues that participatory design should center those who are most exposed, rather than merely balancing the interests of all parties."
  - "Responsibility should be distributed among designers, companies, regulators and users without being diluted. Transparency reports, algorithm audits and whistleblower protection help keep this discussion open."
limitations: "This is an argumentative text with no empirical study: the cases are imagined scenarios. The examples come from elder care, autonomous vehicles and policing, not from manufacturing, and the author himself asks whether the position he defends is achievable."

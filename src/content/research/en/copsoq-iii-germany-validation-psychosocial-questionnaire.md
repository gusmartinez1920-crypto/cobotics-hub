It matters to anyone who needs a tested instrument to measure psychosocial factors at work, for example as a picture of the situation before a workstation is changed. The questionnaire has no scales on interaction with machines or trust in automated systems, and that is useful information too."
method: "A validation study with data from 257,236 participants in 966 psychosocial risk assessments carried out in companies across Germany between January 2015 and February 2020, mostly online, with an average participation rate of 61.4%. The authors examined the consistency of the scales, floor and ceiling effects, correlations between scales, the structure through exploratory factor analysis, and the ability to explain outcomes such as satisfaction and burnout through regression. They also compared occupational areas."
findings:
  - "The German version has 84 items and 31 scales. Almost 70% of the items are identical to those in the 2005 version, which preserves comparison with older data. Filling it in online took 24 minutes on average."
  - "Only three scales had low internal consistency: the one on the dissolving of boundaries between work and private life, the one on freedom over breaks and vacation, and the one on feedback about the work done."
  - "Job satisfaction was the outcome best explained by the factors measured (up to 60% of the variation), followed by engagement, burnout and intention to leave. General health, presenteeism and inability to relax stayed at around 20%."
  - "Conflict between work and private life appeared in all seven regression models and was the main associated factor in all the health models."
  - "Emotional demands vary considerably between occupational areas, but leadership quality hardly varies. For this reason the authors consider it unrealistic to predict someone's psychosocial conditions from their profession alone."
limitations: "The sample is not random and there is no information about those who did not respond. Content validity rests on the literature and on experts, not on studies with respondents, and the authors ran no test of stability over time and no confirmatory factor analysis, which they leave for future work."

whyItMatters: "Risk assessment of a collaborative cell usually focuses on physical hazards. This model adds stress, fatigue, training, trust in the robot, communication and cybersecurity, in checklists that workers fill out in about 15 minutes. It complements the assessment required by the standards without replacing it."
method: "The authors, from South Korea, selected 45 risk items from three reference models and organized them into three fields (worker, robot systems and work environment) and nine factors. Thirteen experts from manufacturers, users, consultancies and universities ranked the items by pairwise comparison, according to their relevance for preventing robot accidents. The authors then built six checklists of 20 questions each and applied them at six workplaces, four with past robot accidents and two without, with 3 or 4 workers per site."
findings:
  - "In the experts' assessment, the most important field for preventing robot accidents is the worker (44.9%), followed by the work environment (33.8%) and robot systems (21.3%)."
  - "The items with the highest weight are stress and fatigue (5.78%), amount of training and expertise (5.03%) and situational awareness (5.03%). Physical and environmental risks ranked lower than the authors expected."
  - "The six checklists cover personality, psychological state, trust in the robot, organization and procedures, collaboration and communication, and cybersecurity. An average below 3, on a five-level scale, signals that the company needs to act."
  - "Physical and task-related risks did not get a checklist, because conventional methods already assess them."
  - "At the two sites without accidents, no serious problems appeared. At the four with accidents, the authors report vulnerabilities in all three fields that, in their view, existing risk assessments would not identify."
limitations: "The validation is qualitative: the paper gives no scores, statistical tests or reliability measures for the checklists, and refers the details to another paper. The number of companies is small, and the authors acknowledge that they did not control for participants' experience. The weights reflect the opinion of 13 experts, not accident data."

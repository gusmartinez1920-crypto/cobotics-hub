whyItMatters: "A workstation can be safe by the standards and still not feel safe to the person in it. The study shows that this feeling affects willingness to work with the robot and that it depends on trust and on feeling part of the team. It also shows how a virtual environment can be used to test a workstation before it exists."
method: "The authors ran a laboratory experiment with 30 participants from a university in the United States, split into two groups: an area shared with the robot (14) or areas separated by a pile of blocks (16). Wearing a virtual reality headset, each person laid blocks on a wall for 7 minutes next to a virtual bricklaying robot. Questionnaires measured team identification, trust in the robot, perceived safety and intention to work with the robot, and the analysis used statistical mediation models."
findings:
  - "Separating the work areas increased the perceived safety of the task (β = 0.40; p < 0.01)."
  - "The effect runs through two paths: team identification and trust in the robot. With both in the model, the direct effect of separation is no longer significant, and the model explains 59% of the variance in perceived safety."
  - "People who felt safer reported a stronger intention to work with the robot in the future (β = 0.41; p < 0.05)."
  - "Age and video game experience had no effect."
  - "The authors propose separating the areas without blocking the view of the robot, planning the robot's path so it does not cross the worker's, and using the virtual environment as training before the real work."
limitations: "The sample is small and made up of engineering students with no construction experience, in a single 7-minute task with a virtual robot. Person and robot worked side by side, with no contact and no shared task, and the result was not validated with workers on real construction sites."

whyItMatters: "Most cobot research aims to improve the performance of the human-robot pair, not the operator's experience. This review organizes what is known about what tires the mind of someone working with the robot, how to measure it and which solutions have been tested. It also warns about the privacy cost of solutions based on body-worn sensors."
method: "The authors carried out a scoping review in four article databases, with the search run in June 2022 and no year limit. From 165 records, two authors independently selected 23 studies on mental workload in collaboration with cobots in real or simulated industrial settings. Studies dealing only with physical load were left out."
findings:
  - "Of the 23 studies, 17 were done in a lab and only 2 in a factory with workers; 12 had fewer than 20 participants. The tasks were mostly assembly and pick and place."
  - "To measure mental workload, 11 studies used the NASA-TLX questionnaire and 14 used physiological measures, mainly cardiac activity (10 studies) and skin conductance (6). Only 6 studies combined self-report, physiology and performance."
  - "Robot motion matters: high speed combined with proximity increases stress, as do a pace that imposes time pressure and unpredictable movement. Advance warning and cues about what the robot will do reduce stress."
  - "Task organization also counts. When the cobot takes over heavy, repetitive work, the worker is left with mental tasks such as monitoring and troubleshooting. Following the robot's pace is more stressful than leading, although productivity is higher when the robot leads."
  - "The solutions found adjust pace, trajectory or task allocation based on physiological signals. One of them, tested in a company with four workers, proposes a new task allocation that the worker can accept or refuse, and it reduced mental and physical load."
limitations: "The review describes a field that is still fragile: lab studies with volunteers, small samples and short, isolated tasks that do not reflect prolonged factory operation. Results are mixed, and some studies found no increase in workload. The solutions depend on monitoring the worker's body, and the authors call for a clear data policy and the right to refuse without penalty."

whyItMatters: "Productivity studies with robots rarely measure, at the same time, what happens to the body and mind of the people doing the work. This experiment combines both and shows where the load goes when the robot comes in: it leaves the workers and concentrates on the person controlling it. The robot here is a large one used in construction, not a cobot; the link to collaborative cells is for the reader to draw."
method: "An exploratory experiment in a digital fabrication laboratory at TU Braunschweig, over two weeks in August 2024. Two teams of two non-professional workers each produced one beam cast in formwork and one beam of the same outer dimensions made by shotcrete 3D printing, in which an experienced pilot controlled the robot from a separate room. The authors measured time per task, mass carried, heart rate, blood lactate, spine posture with a wearable sensor, perceived exertion (Borg scale) and workload (NASA-TLX). Because of the sample size, they used descriptive statistics only."
findings:
  - "Total time was 159 ± 1 minutes for the formwork process and 59 ± 9 minutes for the robotic process, a 63% reduction. The authors report that other studies found reductions of about 23% to 48%."
  - "Heart rate and lactate stayed low and equal in both processes. Perceived exertion was also similar: 9 to 12 on the Borg scale with formwork and 9 to 10 with the robot."
  - "NASA-TLX workload ranged from 18 to 35 with formwork and from 11 to 18 with the robot. In the formwork process, workers spent on average 38% of the time bent over; assembling and removing the formwork, done on the floor, were the tasks with the longest time in that posture."
  - "The pilot had higher heart rate, mental demand, time pressure and effort than the workers. The authors attribute this to the responsibility of operating the robotic arm safely near people."
  - "Time pressure and frustration were lower with the robot, because the workers set the machine's pace through the pilot instead of being paced by it. The authors conclude that a single indicator describes the worker's response poorly and that several must be combined."
limitations: "The sample is very small (four workers and one pilot), made up of young university students, in a laboratory and over a short period, with no statistical testing. The productivity gain does not include costs, the investment in the robot or the steps left out (rebar bending, mixing and cleaning), and the authors themselves say one cannot directly claim that productivity triples. It is unknown whether the pilot's higher load is a pattern or a trait of that individual."

whyItMatters: "Anyone designing a cobot application faces dozens of standards and does not know where to start. The study proposes an order of priority and shows how the choice changes with the task. It also warns about a common habit: assessing the cobot with the same procedures used for conventional robots."
method: "The authors carried out a narrative literature survey with 40 references, presented at a conference. They identified the standards cited in those works and classified them using the type A, B1, B2 and C structure of ISO 12100. From there they proposed a priority pyramid and grouped case studies by application: assembly, material handling, quality inspection, machining and welding."
findings:
  - "Of the 37 standards classified, 20 are type A (ergonomics, mental workload, postures, risk assessment), 2 are type B1 (control systems), 10 are type B2 (guards, emergency stop, danger signals) and 5 are type C, among them ISO 10218 and ISO/TS 15066."
  - "In the authors' reading, type A standards focus on the person, type B standards lean toward technology (the integrity of the robot and of protective devices) and type C standards deal with collaboration itself. The order of priority is C, then B, then A."
  - "The authors report that organizations leave the application of standards to third-party integrators and, when installing a cobot, use the same safety evaluations as for conventional robots, which they see as a concern for the operator."
  - "According to the authors, the combination of standards and assistive technologies does not seem to follow a structured approach. In assembly, the cobot stands still for physical interaction; in handling heavy parts, the safeguards of traditional automation return, such as sensitive floors and guards."
  - "Machining, welding and quality inspection appear as emerging applications. The authors see inspection as inherently safe and underused."
limitations: "The survey does not say how sources were searched and selected, and it is a short conference paper: it works as a map of standards, not as measured evidence. The claim that traditional automation policies applied to cobots bring risks to the operator is not backed by data. The standards cited are in editions earlier than the current ones."

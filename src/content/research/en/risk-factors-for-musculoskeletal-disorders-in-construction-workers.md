whyItMatters: "The study is not about robots: it is a review of the health of construction workers. The link to cobots is the reader's: the list of factors with strong evidence shows which tasks a collaborative robot or another assistive device should relieve. It also shows that high demand and mental stress matter, and poorly designed automation can increase them."
method: "The authors carried out a systematic review following the PRISMA protocol, registered in advance, in five databases, covering January 2000 to September 2020. Two independent reviewers selected the studies. From 777 records, 20 studies were included, with about 194,863 participants from 11 countries or regions. The quality of each study and the level of evidence for each association were rated using published criteria; there was no meta-analysis because the studies differed too much from one another."
findings:
  - "The 12-month prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders ranges from 25% to 96%. The lower back is the most affected region (38% to 66%), followed by the shoulder (30% to 47%), neck (24% to 44%) and knee (30% to 41%)."
  - "The review found strong evidence for three physical factors: awkward postures (odds ratio of 2.43), manual material handling (2.21) and prolonged work (4.02). Evidence is moderate for overhead work and for the use of vibrating machines."
  - "Among psychosocial factors, evidence is strong for high job demand (odds ratio of 1.63) and mental stress (1.79), and moderate for low job satisfaction. For low job control and job insecurity, it is very limited."
  - "By body region, the standouts are frequent overhead work for the shoulder (odds ratio of 3.66), kneeling for the knee (3.94) and repetitive tasks for the wrist and hand (4.53)."
  - "The authors recommend measuring physical and mental strain in real time, using validated questionnaires and field-tested wearable sensors, and running studies that follow workers over time."
limitations: "Most of the included studies observe workers at a single point in time, so the review shows associations, not cause. Many studies do not define or quantify the physical factors well, and participants are almost all men. It was not possible to separate the factors by trade, and robots appear only as a prevention option mentioned in passing."

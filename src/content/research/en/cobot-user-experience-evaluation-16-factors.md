whyItMatters: "Anyone developing or choosing a cobot needs to know what weighs most on the user experience and where to start improving. The study offers a list of weighted factors and a single score, and indicates that the quality of communication between robot and person lies at the root of several other factors. It also reinforces that safety must be perceived by the user, not merely exist."
method: "The authors started from 48 factors drawn from standards, classic usability questionnaires and studies of human-robot collaboration. Nine experts from academia and industry filtered the list over two consultation rounds and compared the 16 remaining factors pairwise. Two methods for analyzing relationships between factors (DEMATEL and ISM) produced the weights, the cause-and-effect relationships and the hierarchical levels. In the case study, 30 participants used a cobot to carry an acrylic panel and answered a questionnaire, before and after the interface was redesigned."
findings:
  - "The 16 factors were grouped into five categories: effectiveness, satisfaction, efficiency, naturalness and trust. Trust has the highest weight (0.261) and satisfaction the lowest (0.154)."
  - "The highest-weighted factors are ease of use, completeness and safety. Appearance has the lowest weight."
  - "The quality of the information exchanged between person and cobot (intention, planned trajectory, task and internal state) is the factor that most influences the others. The authors recommend focusing design on it, on functional suitability and on interface quality."
  - "Workload, appearance and fluency were isolated in the hierarchy, with little connection to the other factors."
  - "After 20 interface improvements, the total score rose and seven factors showed significant gains, among them interface quality (165%), safety (68.75%) and completeness (51.51%)."
limitations: "The participants had similar educational backgrounds, an average age of 23 and experience in user experience or in cobots; they were not factory operators. The test used a single simple task in a laboratory. The weights come from the judgment of nine experts, and the authors themselves call for more varied scenarios and user groups."

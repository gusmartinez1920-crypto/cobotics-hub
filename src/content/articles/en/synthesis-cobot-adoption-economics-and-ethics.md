The 11 summaries in this topic point to the same answer: the cobot is presented as safe, easy to program, and collaborative, but the factory runs into complexity, ambiguous payback math, and organizational work that nobody planned for. Where deployment was observed up close, the robot works next to people, not with them. The base is small: interviews, case studies, one survey, and models tested in simulation.

## On the factory floor, the cobot almost never truly collaborates

In interviews at 15 Danish companies, none had direct interaction between person and cobot during the operating cycle. The cobot did as much of the task as possible and was seen as a cheap industrial robot ([Wallace, 2021](/en/research/cobot-implementation-danish-companies-emerging-ethics/)). A position paper reads it the same way: high levels of collaboration are common in research and rare in industry, where stop-and-go applications prevail ([Weiss et al., 2021](/en/research/cobots-promises-versus-practice-research-roadmap/)).

## What keeps companies away is perceived complexity

In a survey of managers at 242 small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the Chinese province of Guangdong, perceived complexity was the factor with the greatest weight on the intention to adopt cobots, and the only negative one. Being able to try the robot and to see it working had a positive effect. Pressure from competitors and government support had no significant effect ([Liu & Cao, 2022](/en/research/cobot-adoption-in-smes-perceived-complexity-china/)).

A review of 35 texts names lack of knowledge as the main barrier in more than one study. It describes three conflicting views of safety: the cobot is safe by nature, too safe, because the limits reduce productivity, or less safe, because it brings new risks ([Berx et al., 2022](/en/research/safety-and-low-cobot-adoption-paradoxical-tension/)). Programming is part of the difficulty. In an industrial pilot, two operators with no robotics expertise created a pick-and-place application in less than 10 minutes, using skills prepared by specialists ([Giberti et al., 2022](/en/research/cobot-programming-by-non-experts-small-batches/)).

## The payback math changes depending on what goes into it

At the Danish companies, the return calculation turned out ambiguous: jobs were modified, not eliminated, and removing parts of a job is not the same as saving labor ([Wallace, 2021](/en/research/cobot-implementation-danish-companies-emerging-ethics/)).

Three proposals try to organize the math. One calculates the threshold batch size, above which the robot's setup pays off against manual work ([Giberti et al., 2022](/en/research/cobot-programming-by-non-experts-small-batches/)). Another includes ergonomics: in a simulated case with 26 tasks, the cobot cut the cycle from 553 s to 507 s, and giving ergonomics a monetary value widened the region in which the payback is acceptable ([Faccio et al., 2026](/en/research/cobot-large-parts-picking-assembly-ergonomics-payback/)). The third adds up worker, cobot, station, and gripper costs on an assembly line, but leaves out the purchase and integration of the cobot ([Demiralay & Kara, 2025](/en/research/assembly-line-balancing-cobots-gripper-cost-model/)).

## People and the organization decide the outcome

In a welding automation case, with 12 interviews, operator participation eased the transition. The case revealed a new factor: recording the variability of the manual process before the change ([Charalambous et al., 2015](/en/research/organizational-human-factors-for-introducing-robots/)). In the Danish study, the operator's skill became visible only when someone tried to copy it, and success depended on long cycles of trial and error and on one highly motivated person ([Wallace, 2021](/en/research/cobot-implementation-danish-companies-emerging-ethics/)).

Simplifying the interaction also has a cost. In one industrial project, performance improved, but the operator did not understand the system and could not correct failures ([Weiss et al., 2021](/en/research/cobots-promises-versus-practice-research-roadmap/)). In the laboratory, with 30 participants, the quality of the information exchanged between person and cobot was the factor that most influenced the others ([Li et al., 2026](/en/research/cobot-user-experience-evaluation-16-factors/)). In a workshop with 17 master's students, when voice communication failed, responsibility went back to the person ([Ron et al., 2025](/en/research/workshop-teaching-inclusive-human-robot-collaboration-construction/)).

## What is still unknown

Research on adoption is recent: the empirical studies are qualitative or exploratory, with few observations ([Berx et al., 2022](/en/research/safety-and-low-cobot-adoption-paradoxical-tension/)). The survey measures the intention to adopt, not actual use, in a single province ([Liu & Cao, 2022](/en/research/cobot-adoption-in-smes-perceived-complexity-china/)). The field studies include one case that is not collaboration in the strict sense ([Charalambous et al., 2015](/en/research/organizational-human-factors-for-introducing-robots/)) and a single project run by the authors themselves ([Weiss et al., 2021](/en/research/cobots-promises-versus-practice-research-roadmap/)). The payback figures come from simulation or from values set by the authors ([Faccio et al., 2026](/en/research/cobot-large-parts-picking-assembly-ergonomics-payback/); [Demiralay & Kara, 2025](/en/research/assembly-line-balancing-cobots-gripper-cost-model/)). The user experience tests did not involve factory operators ([Li et al., 2026](/en/research/cobot-user-experience-evaluation-16-factors/); [Ron et al., 2025](/en/research/workshop-teaching-inclusive-human-robot-collaboration-construction/)). On ethics there is little: the broadest review in the set does not discuss adoption barriers, cost, ethics, or regulation ([Baratta et al., 2023](/en/research/human-robot-collaboration-overview-research-themes/)).

## In practice

- Before buying, ask to try the cobot and to see it working, and check the supplier's support: these factors favored the intention to adopt ([Liu & Cao, 2022](/en/research/cobot-adoption-in-smes-perceived-complexity-china/)).
- Involve the operator from the concept stage and record how manual work handles variations in the part ([Charalambous et al., 2015](/en/research/organizational-human-factors-for-introducing-robots/)).
- Check whether the batch size pays for the robot's setup ([Giberti et al., 2022](/en/research/cobot-programming-by-non-experts-small-batches/)) and start with tasks that add no value and wear people out, such as fetching large parts ([Faccio et al., 2026](/en/research/cobot-large-parts-picking-assembly-ergonomics-payback/)).
- Set aside time and people for the trial-and-error phase, and do not count a job that was only modified as a labor saving ([Wallace, 2021](/en/research/cobot-implementation-danish-companies-emerging-ethics/)).
- Assess the safety of the whole system as early as the stage when the purchase is being considered, as one of the reviews proposes ([Berx et al., 2022](/en/research/safety-and-low-cobot-adoption-paradoxical-tension/)).
